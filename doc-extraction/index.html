Welcome. This is the first of a weekly series. Each week we take one AI capability and work out which approaches are worth recommending today, and what they cost. Today: getting content out of documents.

The question

"What is the best way to extract the contents of these PDFs?"

This is a frequent client question. The answer depends on the following factors.

How many?
Fifty contracts can be reviewed manually; five million pages cannot. Volume determines how much the cost per page matters.
Digital or scanned?
A PDF made from Word already contains its text. A scan or photograph contains only an image.
How long, how complex?
Two-column layouts, footnotes, tables that cross pages, and 300-page documents all make it harder.
Tables, charts, handwriting?
These are where most methods fail, and where the newest models have improved most.
How accurate, how checkable?
Is 98% good enough? Do we need to point to where each value came from on the page?
Where can the data go?
Many clients can only use services running in their own Azure, Google Cloud or AWS account.
What is the output for?
Search and RAG need clean text. A database needs specific fields. A review team needs the source location.
Budget and speed?
Prices range from free to about $80 per 1,000 pages. Speed ranges from 1,000 pages a second to several seconds per page.
One-off or repeated?
A one-off job can use a general model. A pipeline that runs every day needs to be cheap and stable.

Ask the room which of these they have been asked about recently. The rest of the session gives us a way to turn these answers into a recommendation.

A framework for comparison

Three questions for comparing approaches

Question 1

Where do the characters come from?

  • From the text already stored in the PDF
  • From a model that recognises characters in an image
  • From a model that reads the page image and generates the text
Question 2

Where does the structure come from?

  • Rules based on where text sits on the page
  • Separate models that find headings, columns and table cells
  • Learned inside one large model, with no separate step
Question 3

What can you check afterwards?

  • The exact position of every character
  • A box and a confidence score for each word or block
  • Sometimes only the final text, with no way to trace it back
Newer approaches generally handle difficult documents better, but cost more and produce output that is harder to verify. Several recent tools add verification features.

Keep these three questions in mind. For each approach we'll answer them. Clients often overlook question 3 until an audit requires it.

Family 1 · PDF text libraries

How PDF libraries reconstruct structure

Good at
  • Exact characters and exact positions, with no recognition errors
  • Milliseconds per page on a laptop, so millions of pages cost almost nothing
  • Completely repeatable and easy to audit
Bad at
  • Scans (there is no text to read)
  • Columns, headers and footnotes in the wrong order
  • Tables without ruling lines, and tables that span pages
  • Maths, and files with broken font maps
On one benchmark a pure text-layer tool scores 20. Adding a small layout model raises it to 44, at 24 pages a second on a CPU.

Family 2 · Classic OCR

How classic OCR works

Clean the imageConvert to black and white. Remove noise and shading.
Straighten itDetect the angle of the text and rotate the page level.
Find the linesLook for horizontal bands of ink.
Find the wordsSplit each line at the gaps. Each word gets a box.
Recognise the charactersSince 2018, Tesseract reads a whole line at once with a small neural network (an LSTM). Each word gets a confidence score.

Click through the five steps. The output is words with boxes and confidences. Classic OCR does not know what a heading, a table or a column is. Errors are small and local, like "l" for "I" or "," for ".". Red marks show low-confidence words, which is where errors cluster.

Tesseract: free, about 3–5 seconds a page on CPU, 100+ languages, poor on handwriting and complex layouts. The approach is still developing: Datalab's new Indra OCR (Oct 2026) is a 1M-parameter classic-style model doing 49 pages a second on CPU.

Two ideas from research

Two influential research results

2019 · LayoutLM, Microsoft

Using word positions as model input

A number to the right of "Total" is probably the total. Feeding positions into a language model made form and invoice extraction much better.

2021 · Donut, NAVER Clova

Generating output directly from the image

This switched the task from recognising characters to generating text. It handles difficult pages better, but it can also produce text that is not on the page.

Meta's Nougat (2023) demonstrated this risk: on unfamiliar pages it repeated itself or generated text that was not on the page. Current generative models carry the same risk.
Family 4 · Specialist document models

Three designs of specialist model

Design A

A pipeline of small models

Separate models for layout, tables, formulas and OCR, joined by code. Uses the PDF's own text where it can.

Docling · Marker "fast" · MinerU pipeline · PP-StructureV3
Design B

Layout first, then read each block

Find the blocks on a small, low-resolution copy of the page. Then read each block at full resolution.

MinerU 2.5 · PaddleOCR-VL · Surya 2 · Marker 2
Design C

A single model converts the whole page

A small vision language model (1–9 billion parameters) takes the page image and outputs the full page as markdown or HTML, often with block positions.

Chandra · olmOCR · dots.ocr · DeepSeek-OCR · Mistral OCR

Images: IBM Docling (MIT), OpenDataLab MinerU2.5 (arXiv 2509.22186), Datalab Chandra 2 blog. Click to zoom.

A is the most checkable and cheapest; C is the simplest and handles the messiest pages; B aims to combine the strengths of both. Many products mix designs: Marker uses A for clean digital pages and only calls a model where text is broken.

Family 4 · Inside a pipeline

The stages of a document pipeline

Find every line of textA detection model marks where text is, without reading it yet.
Label the blocksA layout model tags each region: section header, text, footnote, page header, table, figure.
Decide the reading orderNumber the blocks in the order a person would read them. This resolves the multi-column problem.
Read the textOCR each line, or reuse the PDF's own text where it is good.
Rebuild the tablesFind rows, columns and cells, then place the text in them.

Images: Datalab Surya README (GitHub). Surya 2 (May 2026) now does layout, OCR and tables in one 650M-parameter model, and gives each block a confidence score.

This is Datalab's Surya on the first page of the "Attention Is All You Need" paper. Each step produces an output that can be checked: a box, a label or an order number.

Family 4 · Reducing cost

Reducing the number of tokens per page

DeepSeek-OCR (Oct 2025) tested how few tokens are needed to represent a page.

64–400
image tokens per page, depending on mode
~97%
accuracy with up to 10× compression
200k
pages a day on one A100 GPU

Compare a general model, which uses 560 to 2,700 image tokens per page. Fewer tokens reduce cost and processing time. Together with smaller models, this explains why self-hosted costs are now $0.10–0.70 per 1,000 pages in GPU time.

MinerU 2.5 uses a similar method: it finds the layout on a 1036×1036 downsized page, then reads only the text regions at full resolution. This keeps the overall analysis cheap and applies full resolution only where needed.

Self-host cost is GPU time only: publisher throughput at $2.50 per GPU hour. Engineering and licences are extra.

Family 5 · General vision language models

How a general model reads a page

1. Cut into patchesThe page image is split into small squares, 28–32 pixels across.
→
2. Patches become tokensA vision encoder turns patches into "image tokens".
→
3. The model generates textThe language model produces the output text one token at a time.
The model has no separate character recognition step. Reading, layout and interpretation all take place through next-token prediction. As a result, it handles handwriting and charts well but can produce fluent text that is not on the page.

The model never sees characters, only pixels. Gemini and Claude also get the PDF's embedded text alongside the image when you send a PDF directly, which helps.

Family 5 · Weaknesses

Weaknesses of general models

On a 121-page set of deliberately hard pages, Datalab found that a single pass of a raw model produced errors or degenerate output on about 61% of pages. With retries and checks, this fell to nearly zero.
Datalab anti-hallucination blog, vendor-run
What happensWhy
It corrects the textThe model favours likely words, so it corrects typos, normalises dates and fills gaps.
It leaves things outIt hits its output limit, skips repeated rows or summarises. There is no error message.
It repeats itselfIt can loop on dense or unusual pages.
It misreads small printThe page image is reduced to fit the model's size limit, which can make small text illegible.
Errors on long documentsWhen many pages are sent in one request, content from different pages can be mixed up. Send one page at a time.
No confidence scoresThe output does not show which parts are uncertain, so reviewers cannot tell where to check. If asked for word or block locations, the model generates approximate coordinates rather than measuring them from the page.
Results changeProviders update models under the same name, and outputs can differ between runs even at temperature 0.

The main difference from OCR: OCR errors are small and local (a wrong character). Generative errors are fluent and plausible (a whole believable number). The second kind is much harder to spot in review.

Recent developments

Verification features in recent tools

  • Use the PDF's own text when it is good. Only read pixels where it is missing or broken. (Marker, Docling, Gemini and Claude with PDF input)
  • Detect layout at low resolution and read text at full resolution. (MinerU, PaddleOCR-VL)
  • Small model to transcribe, large model only for meaning. (Marker with an LLM, Google Layout Parser)
  • Check, retry and flag uncertainty. Re-read doubtful blocks and mark text "[illegible]" instead of guessing. (Datalab)
  • Return source positions. Mistral OCR 4, Chandra and Docling all return boxes, so a reviewer can see where each value came from.

For clients in regulated work, this is the most relevant development. "Show me where on the page this number came from" is now possible with most modern tools, not only the cloud services.

Results on other vendors' benchmarks differ. Datalab is 45th on LlamaIndex's ParseBench. LlamaParse is 5th on Datalab's OmniParseBench.

Limitations

  • Tuning to the benchmark. Datalab moved its small Surya model from 23rd to 3rd on ParseBench with 70 training steps. A prompt change gave another vendor 22.6 points.
  • Saturation. Top systems on olmOCR-Bench are within 2 points; on OmniDocBench within 1. Differences of this size are within noise.
  • Unrepresentative documents. Most are English, academic or from the publisher's own customers.
  • Model involvement in test creation. Gemini and Claude helped write some OmniParseBench tests.
  • Limited cost data. Only vendor boards show it, from single runs at list price.
  • Few long documents. Most test single pages. Plain model calls score 5–38 on ExtractBench's long documents.

Who to watch regularly: OmniParseBench (Datalab, versioned), ParseBench and ExtractBench (LlamaIndex, live CSVs on GitHub), OmniDocBench (roughly quarterly), OCRBench v2 (quarterly, private set). Read them as evidence, not verdicts.

  • Why not blend extraction? The extraction benchmarks share too few systems to put on one scale.
  • Ties. Treat differences under about 1–2 points as a tie.
05 · Where the frontier is

Frontier: everyday reliability

Points to make: the spread is only about 8 points among serious systems. Self-hosted small models (LightOnOCR, dots.mocr) and Claude Haiku 5.5 sit on the frontier at $0.10–2 per 1,000 pages. Try the "Available on Azure, GCP or AWS" filter: the frontier becomes Claude Haiku → LlamaParse (Marketplace) → Claude Opus.

Note the Datalab vs ParseBench dispute: Datalab scores 93 on its own plain-page slice, 84 on ParseBench's content score.

05 · Where the frontier is

Frontier: hard pages

Datalab accurate at about $10 is the cheapest system in the top group. General models (Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash) match or beat it but cost $30–35 per 1,000 pages as benchmarked, with thinking on. Small open models drop 10–30 points here, which is the price of self-hosting cheaply.

Charts are not in this blend. On ParseBench, products without a chart-to-table step score 1–10 on charts, while general models score 30–70.

05 · Where the frontier is

Conclusions

1
Simple pages. On typical pages, the best and the low-cost options are within a few points. An option costing $0.10–4 is usually sufficient.
2
Difficult pages. Handwriting, scans and complex tables separate systems by 10–30 points. Route those pages to a stronger, more expensive option.
3
Cloud availability. The cloud providers' own document services are rarely on the frontier, but general models served through those clouds often are. Claude is available on all three; Gemini on Google Cloud; GPT and Mistral OCR on Azure.
4
"Thinking" settings. These change the cost by 5–10×. Gemini 3.8 Flash costs about $7 per 1,000 pages with low thinking and $34 with high, for about 1–2 points.
5
Long documents. For fields from 100-page contracts, use a pipeline or agent that splits, extracts and checks. A single model call is not sufficient.

06 · Before recommending

Testing on client documents

Step 1

Sample

Take 50–200 pages that match the real mix. Include the most difficult 10%, such as faxes, handwritten notes and long schedules.

Step 2

Create reference answers

Only for the fields, totals and tables the client will use, not for every word.

Step 3

Test three options

A cheap baseline, the default pick and a premium option. Use the same pages and settings for all.

Step 4

Measure

Field accuracy, table accuracy, pages that failed silently, cost at the real volume, and speed.

Step 5

Route

Send simple pages to a low-cost option, difficult pages to a stronger option, and low-confidence results to human review.

Step 6

Repeat

Re-run every quarter. Prices and model quality change every few months.

Benchmarks help select the options to test. The final choice should be based on results from the client's documents.

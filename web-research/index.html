Second session in the series. Last week was getting content out of documents. This week is getting information off the web: search engines, what websites are made of, how sites control bots, and how research agents have developed, including why the first deep research products differed so much.

The question

"Can AI research this for us?"

Clients ask this about market sizing, competitor scans, supplier checks and literature reviews. The answer depends on the following factors.

What kind of question?
A single fact, a hard-to-find fact, a long list of similar facts, or a written report. Each needs a different tool.
How many?
One report a week can use a premium agent. 100,000 company lookups need a cheap search API and a fixed process.
Which sources?
The open web, paywalled databases, or the client's own files. A tool can only use what it can reach.
How current?
Search indexes are hours to weeks behind. Some questions need the page as it is today.
How checkable?
Does every claim need a link to the passage that supports it? Will someone need to repeat the work later?
Who sets the method?
Can the tool choose its own approach, or must the work follow a methodology the team has agreed?
Where can the queries go?
A search query reveals what the client is working on. Many clients require queries to stay in their own cloud or with approved providers.
Budget and speed?
From a fraction of a cent and one second per search, to several dollars and half an hour per research task.
Read or act?
Reading pages is one task. Logging in, filling in forms and clicking through a site is another, and much less reliable.

Ask the room which of these they have hit recently. The "where can the queries go" factor surprises people: a list of searches about a target company can itself be confidential.

A framework for comparison

Three questions for comparing tools

Question 1

Where do the results come from?

  • A search engine's own index of the web
  • An index bought from, or scraped from, another company
  • Pages fetched live, or a browser operated in real time
Question 2

Who decides what to search next?

  • Fixed code: one search, then an answer
  • A prompted workflow: plan, search in parallel, then write
  • A model trained to search, read and change course
Question 3

What can you check afterwards?

  • A list of links at the end of the answer
  • A citation for each claim, with the supporting passage
  • The full record of every search made and page read
Newer tools search more deeply and adapt better, but they cost more, take longer and are harder to repeat. The better tools now return the evidence for each claim.

Same structure as last week. Question 2 is the one that explains most of the difference between the deep research products, and we will spend some time on it.

02 · How the web works

How a search engine works

CrawlFetch pages, follow their links, and revisit popular pages more often. Well-behaved crawlers read robots.txt first.
RenderRun the page's JavaScript in a browser, because many pages build their content after loading.
IndexSplit the text into terms and record which pages contain each term. This is an inverted index.
Retrieve and rankFind candidate pages for the query, then order them. Keyword scores, link signals and neural models all contribute.
ServeReturn titles, links and snippets. An API returns the same results as data.

The index (6 pages)

Postings for this query

Keyword ranking (BM25)

Meaning-based ranking

BM25 is computed live on these six pages. The meaning-based scores use small hand-made topic vectors to stand in for a real embedding model.

Click through the five stages, then try the three queries. Keyword ranking wins when the query uses the exact name of a scheme. Meaning-based ranking wins when the query is a paraphrase. Real engines combine both, plus link signals such as PageRank, and many other features.

The key point for research agents: an agent can only find what the underlying index contains and ranks highly. If the index is shallow or stale, the agent is too.

The economics: a search engine crawls pages and sends visitors back. An AI model crawls pages for training or answers and sends almost nobody back. Sites responded by blocking, and Cloudflare, which sits in front of more than a quarter of all websites, turned that into product features.

For our work this means: a growing share of good sources will refuse plain fetches from AI tools. While compiling this session's research, openai.com and perplexity.ai themselves returned a Cloudflare challenge (HTTP 403) to a plain request.

Family 3 · Testing an explanation

Why OpenAI's deep research led in early 2025

"OpenAI's was a single thread that could change course. Google's and others' split the work into parallel searches, which suited lists of independent facts but not a task with a set method."
Part of the explanationVerdict
OpenAI's was one loop that adapted as it wentHolds. One model planned, searched and backtracked in a single trajectory.
That is why it was betterMostly not. It ran on an early o3, trained end to end with reinforcement learning on browsing tasks. Gemini's launched on Gemini 1.5 Pro, which was not a reasoning model.
Google's was heavily parallelPartly. A planner and task models, with some sub-tasks run "simultaneously" and others "sequentially". The plan screen looked like a list of independent searches.
Splitting dependent work hurtsHolds, strongly. Google Research found multi-agent setups lost 39–70% on sequential tasks. Anthropic and Cognition say the same.
Parallel designs ignored your methodMixed. Gemini's editable plan was the one place to set a method up front. Its weakness: the plan is fixed before any evidence arrives.
Evidence for the model explanation. Once Gemini Deep Research moved to Gemini 2.5 Pro (April 2025), it beat OpenAI's on the independent DeepResearch Bench (48.9 against 47.0), with the same plan-first design.
OpenAI's edge was mainly a model trained to research. Splitting a task whose steps depend on each other does hurt, but running the same good agent several times in parallel helps. Both vendors now offer an editable plan and steering during the run.

This tests a common explanation, which I held myself: that OpenAI's deep research was better because it was one thread. Step through the five parts. The failure mode is right: splitting a chained task across agents hurts, and every source agrees, including Anthropic, which built the most parallel system. The cause is mostly wrong: OpenAI had the best reasoning model of early 2025, trained end to end on research tasks. When Google upgraded the model, its product caught up without changing the design.

Also: the clarifying questions in ChatGPT came from a separate lighter model, not the research model. OpenAI added plan editing in February 2026 and mid-run steering in November 2025, so the designs have converged.

Sources: research pack D (OpenAI launch post and system card; Google blog posts; Anthropic and Cognition engineering posts; arXiv 2512.08296; DeepResearch Bench, arXiv 2506.11763).

Family 3 · Deep research agents

Two kinds of parallel work

Split one task into pieces

Separate agents take separate parts

Image: Cognition, "Don't Build Multi-Agents", June 2025.

  • Helps when the parts are independent: Anthropic's lead agent with subagents scored 90% higher than one agent on its internal research tests, using about 15 times the tokens of a chat.
  • Hurts when parts depend on each other: each agent makes its own assumptions, and the combining step inherits the conflicts.
Run the whole task several times

Independent full attempts, then pick the best

OpenAI deep research on BrowseComp, 1 to 64 attempts. Image: Wei et al., OpenAI, arXiv 2504.12516.

  • Nothing is split, so no dependency is broken. Each attempt keeps its whole context.
  • The top 2026 settings all do this: GPT Pro, Grok Heavy, Gemini Deep Think and Deep Research Max. It multiplies the cost.
Rule of thumb: run reading and independent look-ups in parallel. Keep decisions that depend on each other in one context.

The useful distinction is not single thread against parallel. It is splitting a task, which breaks dependencies, against repeating a task, which does not. Anthropic's own analysis found that token use alone explained 80% of the variation in BrowseComp scores: much of the multi-agent gain is spending more tokens without overflowing one context window.

A less obvious cost: Anthropic found multi-agent runs were about 3.7 times as likely as single-agent runs to reach an answer by an unintended route, such as a leaked answer key.

Family 3 · How they are trained

How labs train models to search

Start from an obscure factPick a little-known entity, for example from Wikidata.
Walk the web outwardsFollow links across real pages, collecting related facts that each narrow the answer.
Blur the cluesA date becomes "in the early 2010s". A name is partly masked. A number becomes "less than 1%".
Check it is hardKeep the question only if strong models fail it and the answer is not in the first search results.
Reward the right answerLet the model search freely, then reward it only if the short final answer matches. Over many runs it learns its own search strategy.
A published example (BrowseComp)
"What's the title of the scientific paper published in the EMNLP conference between 2018–2023 where the first author did their undergrad at Dartmouth College and the fourth author did their undergrad at University of Pennsylvania?"

Answer: Frequency Effects on Syntactic Rule Learning in Transformers. Easy to check with a few searches; finding it by brute force means examining thousands of papers. The question writer started from the answer and worked backwards.

If the reward only checks the final answer, any route to it counts, including a leaked answer key. Benchmark gains partly measure how closely training data copies the benchmark's style.

Recipe from Alibaba's WebSailor (arXiv 2507.02592), Tongyi DeepResearch (arXiv 2510.24701), Moonshot's Kimi-Researcher and OpenAI's deep research system card. Example question from the BrowseComp paper (arXiv 2504.12516).

This is the "reinforcement learning on hard-to-find internet data" story. The labs built factories for BrowseComp-style questions: obscure, multi-step, with one checkable answer. That makes the reward automatic, which makes reinforcement learning cheap. It is why scores on this kind of benchmark rose so fast. It also explains the limit: the models are trained on puzzles with an answer key, while real research rarely has one.

The main difference from search engines: a search engine gives you sources and you write the answer. A research agent writes the answer and gives you sources, and the link between the two is where most errors hide. Spot-check citations, not prose.

Recent developments

Features that make research checkable

  • The supporting passage for each claim. Claude's search tool returns the cited text with each citation; Parallel's Task API returns a source, reasoning and confidence for each field.
  • Source controls. Allowed and blocked domains and date ranges in search APIs. ChatGPT deep research can be limited to chosen sites (since February 2026).
  • A plan you approve and can change. Gemini Deep Research from launch; ChatGPT since February 2026, with steering during the run.
  • Tables instead of prose. Research APIs that fill a schema, one row per entity with a source per cell, are easier to check than a report.
  • A record of the run. API versions return every search made and page read, so a reviewer can retrace the work.
  • Connectors to private sources. Research tools can now search the client's own files and licensed databases alongside the web, through connectors such as MCP.
For client work, prefer a table with a source per cell over a report. It turns "is this report right?" into a set of checks someone can actually do.

Sources: Anthropic web search tool documentation, Parallel pricing and documentation, OpenAI release notes (February 2026), Gemini API deep research documentation. Research packs B and D.

05 · Are models now better than people?

How BrowseComp works

  • 1,266 questions written by OpenAI's trainers in 2025. Each has a short answer that is easy to check.
  • Written backwards. Start from a fact, then describe it through several obscure clues.
  • Checked for difficulty. GPT-4o, o1 and early deep research could not answer it, and the answer was not on the first page of five Google searches.
  • Graded automatically by comparing the final answer with the reference.

05 · Are models now better than people?

Has training on hard search made models superhuman?

Yes, at finding one hard fact
  • 90–94% on BrowseComp against 29% for human trainers given two hours each.
  • Questions that look like BrowseComp are now generated automatically and used for reinforcement learning, so the skill is trained directly.
  • Humanity's Last Exam with tools rose from 27% (Feb 2025) to 68% (Oct 2026).
  • Caveat: with no tools at all, models answer 24–45% of BrowseComp from memory, so part of the score is recall.
No, at research as a job
  • Completeness. WideSearch: the best agent filled 5% of tables completely; one person managed 20%.
  • Citations. 40–80% of claims supported by the source cited (DeepTRACE).
  • Expert rubrics. Under 68% compliance for Gemini and OpenAI deep research (ResearchRubrics).
  • Calibration. Deep research had the worst confidence calibration OpenAI measured.
Better than people at finding one hard fact. Not yet reliable at producing complete, accurately cited research.

The eval-awareness case: in a multi-agent run on all 1,266 questions, two runs found and decrypted the answer key; one used 40.5 million tokens. Multi-agent runs were about 3.7 times as likely as single-agent runs to find unintended answers. Anthropic's re-run after blocking those sources moved the score from 86.8% to 86.6%.

No published study blind-compares deep research reports with reports written by expert analysts on the same brief. Anthropic reported in October 2026 that its newest model produced earnings reports with no invented figures in 16 of 18 runs, where earlier models managed none.

Limitations

  • Memorisation. With no tools at all, models answer 24–45% of BrowseComp. Exa estimates about half of several hard-search sets are memorised.
  • Saturation. Short lookups score 91–98%; BrowseComp 90–94%. Anthropic stopped reporting BrowseComp in September 2026.
  • Judges. Report benchmarks moved by about 30% when the judge model changed.
  • Small samples. Many vendor charts use 50–100 questions, so ±5–7 points of noise.
  • Cost definitions differ. List price, billed cost or measured spend. Compare cost only within one board.
  • Live web. Sites and facts change, so results drift and runs are hard to repeat.

Cross-checks are informative: on Exa's own chart, Claude Opus 4.8 beats Exa's agent on BrowseComp (84 against 74). On Parallel's September chart, Perplexity ties Parallel at 74% for less money. On You.com's charts, Parallel beats You.com. Source: research pack C, section 6.

05 · Where the frontier is

Frontier: search APIs

Artificial Analysis gives one agent (GPT-5.6 Luna) a different search provider each time. With no search it scores 33; every provider at least doubles that. The frontier: TinyFish at about $9 per 1,000 tasks, Octen at $24, Exa lite at $44 and Exa auto at $66. Perplexity's medium setting is effectively tied with Exa at the top. The cost includes the agent's tokens, so a better search that needs fewer turns can be cheaper overall.

Switch to the Openbenchmarks views: Exa's deep search leads both, and TinyFish is again the cheapest point. None of these providers runs inside Azure, AWS or Google Cloud; AWS's AgentCore Web Search has not been tested on a public board.

05 · Where the frontier is

Frontier: models with a search tool

Parallel's board gives 48 models the same search tool. The frontier runs from Claude Haiku 5.5 (63 at $24 per 1,000 tasks) and MiniMax M3.1 Flash (69 at $44) through GPT-6.1 Sol (70 at $130) to Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 (74–75 at $800–950). 05 · Where the frontier is

Conclusions

1
Search matters, and it is cheap. Adding a search API roughly doubles an agent's score on hard questions (33 to 65–80 on the Artificial Analysis index). Providers differ by a few points, and in these tests most of the cost was the model's tokens, not the searches.
2
Small models search well. Claude Haiku 5.5 and similar models reach most of the top score at a few percent of the cost. Pay for a large model when the reasoning is hard, not for the searching.
3
The last few points are expensive. On BrowseComp, about 84% costs $1–4 per task and about 93% costs $18–95, mostly from running several agents at once.
4
Finding is solved better than reporting. Hard single facts are near the ceiling. Complete lists and accurate citations are not, and no benchmark compares reports with an expert's.
5
The client's cloud decides more than the benchmark. AWS and Google Cloud have web search inside the cloud; Azure does not. None of the leading search APIs on the independent board runs inside any of the three.

06 · Before recommending

Testing on client questions

Step 1

Collect real questions

30–50 questions from recent work, including the hardest 10%, some list tasks and some that depend on recent events.

Step 2

Write answer keys

For facts, the answer and its source. For lists, the complete list. For reports, a checklist of points an expert would include.

Step 3

Test three options

A cheap baseline (search API and a small model), the default pick, and a premium option. Same questions and settings for all.

Step 4

Measure

Correct answers, items missed, a sample of citations checked against their sources, cost per answer, time, and how often two runs disagree.

Step 5

Check where data goes

Where queries are processed, what is logged and for how long, and whether results may be stored. Confirm with the client.

Step 6

Date and repeat

The web changes, so record when each answer was found. Re-run every quarter: prices and models change every few months.

Benchmarks help select the options to test. The final choice should rest on results from the client's own questions.
